Renters Insurance: The $15/Month Coverage Most Renters Skip — and Regret

55% of renters don't have it. Here's why that's a financial mistake that can cost thousands.

📅 Updated August 2026  ·  12 min read  ·  Renters Insurance
Written and researched by Brenda Kay · Editorial Policy

Renters insurance is the most underused protection in personal finance. It's cheap — often $10–$20/month — covers far more than most people realize, and yet roughly half of all renters don't have it. The Complete Guide to Renter's Rights is an excellent resource for understanding the full scope of renter protections available to you. The most common reason people give: "My landlord has insurance on the building." That's true. And it covers exactly zero of your stuff.

Here's everything renters insurance actually covers, what it costs, and why the math makes it one of the easiest financial decisions you'll ever make.

The Biggest Myth: Your Landlord's Insurance Covers You

Your landlord's insurance covers the building — the walls, roof, floors, plumbing, and structure. It does not cover any of your personal belongings, and it does not protect you from liability if someone is hurt in your apartment. For a comprehensive guide to renters' rights and protections, The Complete Guide to Renter's Rights covers everything tenants need to know. When you hear "the building is insured," that means if the building burns down, the landlord can rebuild it. Your laptop, furniture, clothing, and everything else inside? Gone.

What Renters Insurance Actually Covers

Personal Property

This is the main coverage people think of — your belongings: furniture, electronics, clothing, kitchen gear, bikes, sports equipment, instruments. Standard policies cover theft, fire, water damage from plumbing (not floods), vandalism, windstorm, and several other named perils.

Crucially, renters insurance often covers your stuff even when it's not in your apartment. Laptop stolen from your car? Bike stolen from a rack outside? In many cases, your renters policy covers those too, up to your personal property limit.

Liability Coverage

If a guest slips and falls in your apartment and decides to sue you, your renters policy covers legal defense and damages up to your liability limit — typically $100,000–$300,000. This protection is enormously valuable. Personal injury lawsuits are expensive; without liability coverage, a single incident could result in a judgment that takes years to pay off.

Loss of Use

If your apartment becomes uninhabitable due to a covered loss — a kitchen fire, serious water damage — your renters insurance pays for temporary housing (hotel, short-term rental) and additional living expenses while your place is being repaired or while you find a new one. This can be thousands of dollars of coverage for a short displacement period.

Medical Payments to Others

Minor medical bills for guests injured in your home, typically $1,000–$5,000, covered regardless of fault. Designed to handle small situations before they become lawsuits.

What Does It Actually Cost?

The national average for renters insurance is about $14–$18/month for $30,000 in personal property coverage and $100,000 in liability. That's less than most people spend on streaming services — and it covers stuff worth far more. Your coverage cost depends on:

  • Location (urban vs. rural, crime rates, weather risk)
  • How much coverage you need (the value of your belongings)
  • Your deductible (higher deductible = lower premium)
  • Whether you bundle with auto insurance (can save 5–15%)
  • Whether you pay annually vs. monthly

Actual Cash Value vs. Replacement Cost

When shopping for renters insurance, check whether the policy pays Actual Cash Value (ACV) or Replacement Cost Value (RCV) for personal property.

  • Actual Cash Value: pays you what your items are worth today, accounting for depreciation. A 3-year-old laptop might be worth $400 even if you paid $1,200 for it.
  • Replacement Cost Value: pays what it costs to buy the same item new. That same laptop would be covered for $1,200.

Replacement cost policies cost a bit more — maybe $3–$5/month extra — but the difference in a claim can be significant. RCV coverage is almost always worth the small additional premium.

What Renters Insurance Doesn't Cover

  • Floods: Separate flood insurance required, just like homeowners
  • Earthquakes: Separate policy or endorsement needed in seismic areas
  • Roommate's stuff: Only the named insured is covered; roommates need their own policies
  • High-value items above sub-limits: Jewelry, expensive camera equipment, collectibles may be capped — add a rider if needed
  • Business property: Items used for business purposes are often excluded or limited

How to Inventory Your Belongings (and Why It Matters)

Before you can buy the right amount of renters insurance, you need to know how much your stuff is worth. Most people dramatically underestimate this. A realistic inventory for a one-bedroom apartment often reaches $20,000–$35,000 once you add up everything carefully.

The easiest way to build a home inventory:

  1. Walk through room by room with your phone camera — video every drawer, closet, and shelf. Store the video in cloud backup (not just on the device you might lose in a claim).
  2. List high-value items separately — electronics, instruments, bikes, jewelry, cameras. Note the purchase price and current value for each.
  3. Keep receipts in a digital folder — email confirmations, Amazon orders, and warranty registrations are all useful at claim time.
  4. Update annually — as you acquire new things, add them. An outdated inventory that doesn't reflect your current belongings leaves you underinsured.

Some insurers offer home inventory apps or spreadsheets to make this process faster. The 90-minute investment is worth it: a complete inventory speeds up claims, reduces disputes with your insurer, and ensures you're buying the right coverage amount.

When Is Renters Insurance NOT Worth It?

Renters insurance makes financial sense for almost everyone, but there are a few edge cases where the math is tighter:

  • You own almost nothing: If your total possessions are worth under $2,000 and you have no liability concerns, the calculus is closer. But liability coverage alone is often worth the price.
  • Very short-term rental: Staying somewhere for 1–2 months? Your existing policies (credit card travel benefits, homeowners rider) might provide temporary coverage.
  • Items already covered elsewhere: Some high-end credit cards cover travel theft and certain personal property. Check what you already have before assuming you have nothing.

These exceptions are genuinely rare. For anyone with furniture, a laptop, a phone, and normal household goods — and especially anyone with significant savings or assets that could be targeted in a liability claim — renters insurance is almost universally worth it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Does renters insurance cover my roommate's belongings?

No. Renters insurance covers the named insured(s) on the policy only. If your roommate wants coverage, they need their own policy. Some insurers let you add a domestic partner or roommate to a policy, but they typically need to be listed explicitly.

Q: Does renters insurance cover my car?

No — your car itself requires auto insurance. However, renters insurance may cover personal items stolen from your car (like a laptop or camera left in your vehicle), typically up to your personal property limit.

Q: What's the difference between a claim and a loss?

A loss is the event (the break-in, the fire). A claim is the formal request you file with your insurer to be compensated for the loss. Before filing, consider whether the loss exceeds your deductible by enough to make a claim worthwhile — small claims can affect your renewal rate.

Q: Will filing a claim raise my rates?

Possibly, especially for repeat claims. One claim in 3–5 years rarely causes a significant increase. Multiple claims or a pattern of losses can flag you as a higher-risk policyholder and raise your premiums or cause non-renewal.

Q: Can I get renters insurance if I have a dog?

Yes, but some breeds are excluded or require a higher premium. Breeds commonly restricted include pit bulls, Rottweilers, and certain others. If your dog bites someone and your policy has a breed exclusion, the liability claim may not be covered. Disclose your dog breed when getting quotes.

Q: Is renters insurance tax-deductible?

Generally not for personal residences. If you use part of your rental space exclusively for business (home office), a proportional share of renters insurance may be deductible as a business expense. Consult a tax professional for your specific situation.

💡 Bottom line: Renters insurance is one of the best dollar-for-dollar financial decisions you can make. $173/year on average to protect thousands of dollars of belongings, plus liability coverage that could protect you from a financial disaster. If you rent and don't have it, get it today.

Renters Insurance for College Students, Remote Workers, and Special Situations

Standard renters insurance advice assumes a typical apartment renter. But many people have situations that change the calculus — and the coverage that makes sense.

College Students

Students living in dorms are often partially covered under their parents' homeowners or renters policy — typically at 10% of the policy's personal property limit. If parents have $50,000 in personal property coverage, the student gets $5,000 in off-premises coverage. That's usually enough for dorm belongings. Students in off-campus apartments are a different story: they're treated as independent renters and need their own policy. Campus renter policies from insurers like GradGuard are available for as little as $8–$12/month specifically for students.

Remote Workers

If you work from home and have employer-owned equipment (company laptop, monitors, etc.), verify whether your employer's commercial policy or your renters policy covers those items if stolen or damaged. Many renters policies limit or exclude business property coverage — equipment used for business purposes may have a low sub-limit ($500–$2,500) or require a business endorsement. Privately owned home office equipment (your personal laptop you also use for work) is typically covered as personal property, but high-value items should be scheduled separately.

Short-Term Rental Hosts (Airbnb, VRBO)

Standard renters insurance typically excludes liability and property damage claims arising from short-term rental activity. If you rent out a room or your entire apartment on Airbnb, your personal renters policy almost certainly doesn't cover incidents that happen when paying guests are present. Airbnb's AirCover provides some host protection, but its coverage has limitations and exclusions. Dedicated short-term rental endorsements or standalone STR policies are available from insurers like Proper Insurance for hosts who need real coverage.

Pet Owners

Renters insurance liability coverage generally extends to dog bites and pet-related injuries — up to your liability limit. However, many insurers restrict or exclude certain breeds (common exclusions: pit bulls, Rottweilers, German Shepherds, Dobermans). If you have a restricted breed, the liability claim from a bite may not be covered even if you have renters insurance. Always disclose your dog's breed when getting quotes, and specifically ask whether your breed is excluded from liability coverage. Some specialty insurers (like USAA) have more lenient breed policies.

How to File a Renters Insurance Claim: Step by Step

Most people who've never filed an insurance claim don't know what to expect. Here's exactly what the process looks like:

  1. Document the damage or loss immediately. Take photos and videos of everything before touching or cleaning up. For theft, photograph the area where items were taken and evidence of forced entry.
  2. File a police report for theft or vandalism. Your insurer will require a police report number for theft claims. File it the same day — it creates an official record and helps your claim.
  3. Contact your insurer to open the claim. Most insurers have 24/7 claims lines and mobile apps. Opening the claim starts the clock on processing times and locks in the date of loss.
  4. Provide your home inventory. This is why that video walkthrough matters. Submit your inventory, photos, receipts, and serial numbers for claimed items.
  5. Work with the adjuster. The insurer will assign a claims adjuster who reviews your documentation and determines the payout. For ACV policies, they'll calculate depreciation. For RCV policies, you'll receive the replacement cost.
  6. Receive payment and replace items. On RCV policies, you often receive the ACV upfront and the "recoverable depreciation" (the difference between ACV and replacement cost) after you actually replace the item and submit proof of purchase.

The most common claim mistake: not documenting thoroughly before cleaning up. The second most common: not knowing whether you have ACV or RCV coverage until after a loss.

Q: Does renters insurance cover identity theft?

Standard renters insurance policies don't typically include identity theft coverage, but many insurers offer it as an affordable add-on (often $25–$50/year). Identity theft coverage typically provides assistance with fraud monitoring, credit report remediation, legal fees, and lost wages from time spent resolving the theft. Given how common identity theft is and how time-consuming resolution can be, this endorsement is worth considering — especially if you don't already have identity protection through a credit card or standalone service.

Q: What if my landlord requires renters insurance?

An increasing number of landlords require renters insurance as a lease condition — it protects them from liability exposure and reduces the likelihood of tenants being unable to pay for damages they cause. If your landlord requires it, they'll typically ask for a Certificate of Insurance (COI) naming them as an "interested party." Most insurers provide this at no charge. Being listed as an interested party means your landlord is notified if your policy lapses — a simple way for landlords to ensure continuous coverage throughout your tenancy.

Q: Can renters insurance cover hotel costs if I'm displaced?

Yes — this is the Loss of Use (or Additional Living Expenses) coverage. If your apartment becomes uninhabitable due to a covered peril (fire, smoke damage, major water damage), your renters insurance pays for comparable temporary housing while your unit is being repaired or while you find a new place. This includes hotel costs, short-term rental fees, and even the difference in meal costs if your temporary housing doesn't have a kitchen. Coverage is typically capped at 20–30% of your personal property limit, or for a defined period (often 12–24 months).

Q: How do I get the best renters insurance rate in 2026?

Bundle with auto insurance — this single step typically saves 5–15% on both policies. Pay annually rather than monthly to avoid installment fees. Raise your deductible from $250 to $500 or $1,000 (significant premium reduction for a deductible amount that's manageable). Install smoke detectors, deadbolts, and a security system — insurers offer safety discounts of 5–15%. Shop quotes from at least three carriers; apps like Lemonade and Hippo make this fast. The spread between the cheapest and most expensive renters policy for identical coverage can be $100–$150/year — a significant percentage difference on a $150–$200/year policy.
